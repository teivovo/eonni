story first, no biology yet
Before any of the organelles, let me tell you what they actually are, because the textbook lists them like furniture and that is why they will not stick.
A cell is a factory. Not a bag of parts, a factory, with departments in an order.
The nucleus is the office where the plans are kept. Nothing leaves it, so a copy gets sent out instead. The rough ER is the workshop floor. The Golgi is packing and dispatch. The vesicle is the van.
So when a question asks what one department does, it is really asking where that department sits on the road. And when it asks what happens if one is missing, it is asking what the van never receives.

How is biology sitting compared to the maths?
a copying problem
There are two metres of DNA in almost every cell you have. Two metres, in something you cannot see.
Which creates two problems, and the whole of the next few cards is those two problems being solved.
One: how do you pack two metres into a nucleus. That is what the histones are for.
Two: how do you copy the whole thing without losing your place, when the two strands run in opposite directions. That is why one side is built in one long run and the other in short pieces.
Neither is a fact to learn. Both are answers to a problem, and if you can state the problem the answer usually comes with it.
nothing to do with your exam
The lac operon is named after lactose, which you knew. Here is the bit nobody mentions.
We know about it because two men in Paris in the 1950s spent years feeding sugar to bacteria and watching which ones bothered to make the enzyme for it.
That is it. That is the entire experiment. Give the bacteria lactose, see what they switch on. It won a Nobel Prize.
Most of the things in this module that sound intimidating were found out by someone doing something quite boring for a very long time.
a delivery problem
A cell is wrapped in something oily, and most messages in your body are water-loving. So almost every signalling question is the same problem: how does the message get in when it physically cannot get in.
There are only two answers and the whole topic is choosing between them.
If the messenger is oily, it walks straight through and finds its receptor inside. Steroids do this, which is why the receptor is in the cell rather than on it.
If it is not, it never gets in, so it knocks: it binds a receptor on the outside surface and something else carries the message onward from there.
Read the molecule, decide which kind it is, and the answer follows. That is genuinely the whole topic.

a story about rice
You could set fire to a bowl of rice and get all its energy out in about four seconds, as heat and light. Your body has the same amount of energy available and does not do that, for obvious reasons.
So respiration is the same burning, taken apart into about thirty small steps, so the energy comes out in pieces small enough to catch.
That is why there are so many stages, and why each one only releases a little. It is not complexity for its own sake. It is the difference between a fire and a meal.
And it is why the numbers matter: every stage hands off a fixed amount to the next, which is what all those counts of NADH and ATP are recording.
before the graphs
You have probably been told an enzyme is a lock and the substrate is a key. That is nearly right and the small correction is where the marks are.
A real lock does not move. An enzyme does. The substrate arrives, and the enzyme closes round it slightly, like a hand closing on something rather than a slot accepting a coin.
Which explains three exam answers at once. Why heat wrecks it: the shape goes, and the shape was the whole mechanism. Why pH wrecks it: same reason. And why an inhibitor that binds somewhere else entirely can still stop it: bend any part of a shape and the important part moves too.
all of that from one word: induced fit.
you have already seen this one
Catalase turns up in the free radical cards as a name to remember. You have already watched it work, probably more than once.
When you put hydrogen peroxide on a cut and it fizzes white, that is catalase in your cells tearing the peroxide apart into water and oxygen. The fizz is the oxygen.
It is one of the fastest enzymes known. A single molecule of it can get through millions of peroxide molecules a second.
So when a question asks what happens if catalase is broken, you can answer it from having seen it work, rather than from the notes.
last topic, and a short story
The last topic looks like lab trivia. It is the reason the rest of the subject exists, so it gets thirty seconds of context.
Someone noticed that bacteria defend themselves by cutting up invading DNA, and that they always cut at the same short sequence.
Which means you can cut DNA wherever you like, if you know the word to look for. And if two pieces are cut with the same enzyme, their ends match and they stick back together in a new order.
That single fact is the whole of genetic engineering, insulin production, and every DNA test you have ever heard of.
