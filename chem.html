story first, no chemistry yet
Before any calculation, let me tell you what a mole actually is, because the textbook gives you the number and never the reason, and the reason is the easy part.
A dozen is twelve. Not twelve of anything in particular. Twelve eggs, twelve people, twelve problems. It is a counting word.
A mole is the same idea with a much bigger number, and it exists for one reason: you cannot count atoms. They are far too small and there are far too many. So chemists picked a pile size and gave it a name.
The pile was chosen so that the number on the periodic table is the weight of one pile in grams. That is the whole trick, and it is why you can weigh something on a balance and know how many atoms you have.

a story about elbows
Molecules have shapes, and the shapes have names, and being asked to memorise a list of names is why this topic feels arbitrary. It is not arbitrary at all.
Think of a crowded lift. Everyone spreads out as far as the walls allow, without anyone planning it, because nobody wants to be near anyone else.
That is the whole of it. Everything around a central atom repels everything else, so they settle as far apart as they can get, and the shape you end up with is just where that settling stops.
Which means you never have to remember a shape. You count what is around the middle, you spread it out, and you read off what you get.
And the one thing people forget is that lone pairs are in the lift too. You cannot see them in the formula and they take up room anyway.

nothing to do with your exam
Chemists have a holiday for the mole. I am not joking.
It runs on the 23rd of October, from 6:02 in the morning until 6:02 in the evening. Six point zero two, twenty three. Because the number is 6.02 times ten to the twenty third.
There are schools that hold parties. There is merchandise. There are, unavoidably, puns involving small burrowing animals.
I mention it because it is the single easiest way to remember a number you will otherwise blank on, and because it is nice to know that the people who set your paper are, in their own way, enormous nerds.
a story about a cold pack
You have used an instant cold pack, or seen one. You squeeze it, nothing is plugged in, and it goes properly cold in your hand.
Which should be strange. Things that happen by themselves usually give heat out. A fire does. Rusting does. This one takes heat IN, from your hand, and it still happens on its own.
So heat cannot be the only thing deciding whether a reaction goes. There is a second thing, and the whole of this topic is that second thing.
It is how spread out everything ends up. A tidy solid dissolving into a jumble of ions is a big increase in mess, and nature will pay quite a lot of heat for mess.

your own worksheet had the best idea
Your worksheet explains this one with fish, and it is the best analogy anywhere in your three modules, so I am not going to improve on it.
Two tanks, joined by two tunnels. Fish swim left to right through the top one and right to left through the bottom one, and nobody ever stops swimming.
If two go across for every one that comes back, one tank empties. Obvious.
But if one goes each way, the number in each tank stops changing. Not because the fish stopped. Because the two rates became equal.
Hold on to that last sentence, because almost every wrong answer in this topic comes from thinking the reaction has finished. It has not. Nothing has stopped. That is what the word dynamic is doing there.

Last chemistry card.
One thing to carry in with you, and it is the same instinct that runs through the whole module. When a chemistry question looks like it needs something you have not been taught, it almost never does. It needs one thing you have, applied carefully.
Count the gas molecules. Name a, b and c. Ask which one runs out. Work out the determinant first. Every one of those is a first line of working, and a first line of working is worth a mark even when the rest goes wrong.
Write something down. Always write something down.
